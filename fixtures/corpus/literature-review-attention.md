# Introduction {#sec-intro}

Claims about the "ten-minute attention span" of students appear in
teaching handbooks with remarkable regularity, yet the empirical basis
for them is thin [@wilson2007; @bradbury2016, pp. 46--48]. This review
traces where the figure came from and summarises what more recent work
on sustained attention in lectures and seminars actually shows.

We restrict the review to studies published in English, German or
French between 1970 and 2023, conducted with students aged sixteen and
over.[^scope] Laboratory vigilance tasks are discussed only where they
were explicitly used to make claims about classroom behaviour.

[^scope]: Studies of younger children were excluded because the
    measures used (teacher ratings, on-task coding of observable
    behaviour) are not comparable with the self-report and
    probe-caught methods common in adult samples.

# Origins of the ten-minute claim {#sec-origins}

@hartley1978 is frequently cited as the source, but their study
measured note-taking, not attention, and the decline they reported
began after about fifteen minutes rather than ten. @johnstone1976
observed "attention breaks" in chemistry lectures, but the coding
scheme was never published in full and the observers were not blind to
the hypothesis [see @wilson2007, sec. 3].

Bradbury puts it bluntly:

> There is no evidence that a ten- to fifteen-minute limit on student
> attention exists, and a great deal of evidence that attention varies
> with the instructor, the material and the students themselves
> [-@bradbury2016, p. 51].

The persistence of the claim, Bradbury argues, owes more to its
usefulness as a rationale for active-learning techniques than to the
data.^[This is not to say active-learning techniques are ineffective;
only that their justification does not need to rest on a contested
attention figure.]

# Measurement approaches {#sec-measures}

Three families of measures dominate the literature.

Thought probes
:   Students are interrupted at intervals and asked whether their mind
    was on the lecture. Used by @risko2012 and @wammes2016a.

Self-caught reports
:   Students press a clicker whenever they notice their mind has
    wandered. Prone to under-reporting.

Physiological and behavioural indices
:   Eye tracking, EEG, heart-rate variability, or note-taking rate.

Each has known problems. Thought probes may themselves disrupt
attention, and the probe rate interacts with the reported rate of mind
wandering [@seli2013; cf. @robison2019].[^probes]

[^probes]: The interaction appears to be modest. In @seli2013, moving
    from a probe every 45 s to one every 90 s changed the reported rate
    of mind wandering by roughly six percentage points.

    It is worth noting, though, that the faster probe rates used in
    some classroom studies are well outside the range tested there, so
    the size of the effect in those settings is simply unknown.

# What the recent evidence shows {#sec-evidence}

The most careful classroom studies [@wammes2016a; @wammes2016b;
@bunce2010] agree on a few points:

1. Mind wandering is common throughout a lecture, not just after some
   threshold.
2. It tends to increase over the session, but the increase is gradual
   and varies considerably between courses.
3. Brief changes of activity are followed by a temporary drop in
   reported mind wandering.

@bunce2010 in particular found attention lapses as early as the first
thirty seconds, with a pattern of cycling rather than steady decline.
Their sample was small (*n* = 186 across three courses), but the
pattern was consistent.

## Moderators

Interest in the topic, perceived instructor enthusiasm and seating
position have all been reported as moderators [@lindquist2011, p. 3;
@risko2012]. Results on laptop use are mixed, and probably depend on
what the laptop is being used for [@ravizza2017, chap. 2].

# Conclusion {#sec-conclusion}

The ten-minute figure is best regarded as folklore. Attention in
lectures fluctuates from the outset and declines unevenly. Instructors
who vary their activities do appear to help, but not because students
hit a fixed wall at ten minutes.
