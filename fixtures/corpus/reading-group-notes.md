# Session 3: Chang, *Inventing Temperature*, ch. 1 {#session-3}

Seven of us this week. Lucía led the discussion.

## Summary of the chapter

Chang asks how the fixed points of thermometry, and the boiling point
of water in particular, were established when there was no prior
standard to check them against. The short answer is that they were
not established once and for all, but stabilised through a long
process of iteration.

> Water does not boil at a fixed temperature. Or, more precisely, it
> does not boil at a fixed temperature unless we take great care to
> make it do so.
>
> The variations Chang documents include:
>
> + superheating in clean glass vessels
> + "bumping", the sudden violent boiling after superheating
> + dependence on dissolved air
>
> > The 18th-century literature is full of complaints that the boiling
> > point was anything but fixed.
>
> Some of this was known to De Luc by the 1770s.

## Discussion points

+ Is "epistemic iteration" just a fancy name for trial and error?

  Lucía thought not: the point is that each stage builds on the
  previous one and corrects it, rather than starting again. Ravi was
  less convinced, and wanted to know what distinguishes a successful
  iteration from a lucky guess.

+ How does this relate to the coherentism vs. foundationalism debate?

  General agreement that Chang is defending a kind of coherentism, but
  one with a strong temporal element.

+ Does the story generalise beyond thermometry?

  Several suggestions: early pH scales, the standardisation of the
  metre, intelligence testing (more contentious).

## Questions for next time

1) Chang distinguishes "fixed points" from "fixed procedures". Is the
   distinction stable?
2) What role does the instrument maker play, as opposed to the natural
   philosopher?
3) How much of this is specific to the 18th century?
   a. instrument quality
   b. the absence of a theory of heat
   c. the social organisation of science
